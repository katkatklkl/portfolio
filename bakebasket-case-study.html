01 Summary

At a glance

Baking is a hobby that lives in moments — date nights, family afternoons, casual weekends — but buying full-sized ingredients to support those moments feels wasteful and intimidating. Bake Basket sells curated, pre-portioned kits so beginners can bake on impulse, without the leftover commitment.

Problem

Beginners avoid baking because full ingredients feel wasteful and impractical for one-off recipes.

Solution

A mobile-first e-commerce app for one-time, pre-portioned baking kits — no subscription required.

My role

End-to-end ownership: research, IA, wireframes, visual design, prototype, and usability testing.

Outcome

High-fidelity prototype validated through 3 usability tests, with 4 design iterations addressing key friction points.

02 Context & problem

Baking shouldn't require a subscription.

Most baking happens occasionally — for a date night, a family gathering, or a casual afternoon. Yet to participate, beginners need to buy full-sized ingredients for recipes they may only make once. The result: pantries fill up with bottles of vanilla extract and bags of flour that go unused, and people opt out of baking altogether.

Existing meal kit services solve a similar problem for cooking, but they assume routine use through subscriptions — a model that doesn't fit the occasional, social nature of baking.

How might we…

… create a low-effort, low-commitment shopping experience that helps users quickly find and confidently purchase recipe kits without feeling overwhelmed?

03 User research

I conducted semi-structured interviews with five participants aged 20–35 to understand baking motivations, barriers, and behaviors.

Key insights

  • Most participants avoided baking because they didn't want to buy full-sized ingredients they might not reuse.
  • Many felt intimidated by long ingredient lists, unclear steps, and unfamiliar tools.
  • All participants said they'd be more likely to bake if all ingredients were provided and pre-measured.

06 Solution

A simple, playful shopping experience.

Design a streamlined journey — discover → review → cart → checkout — where every step removes friction rather than adding to it. Browsing should feel exciting, not overwhelming. Decisions should be easy to make, easy to revise.

11 Usability testing

Testing the prototype.

I ran an unmoderated usability test with 3 participants on the high-fidelity prototype to surface friction points, validate the purchase flow, and verify navigation clarity.

Key findings

FINDING 01

Filter navigation was confusing

Participants struggled to narrow recipes — unclear which tags were active, no obvious way to reset.

FINDING 02

Trapped on secondary pages

From Saved or Cart, users could only return to Home — forcing unnecessary backtracking between sections.

FINDING 03

Browsing felt overwhelming

Too many filter tags exposed at once during discovery led to decision fatigue before users even reached a recipe.

12 Iterations

Iterating from feedback.

Each iteration addressed a specific friction point uncovered during testing — making the experience clearer, more intuitive, and better matched to how users actually navigate.

ITERATION 01 New filter navigation
Before / After
Filter modal

Tags cluttered the home page on first glance and made the filter system harder to understand. I condensed them into a single Filter button with grouped categories — reducing visual noise so users aren't overwhelmed before they've even started browsing.

ITERATION 02 "Surprise Me" feature
Empty state
Active state

Since the app supports impulse buying, I added a Surprise Me feature that presents 3 randomly selected recipes instead of the full list. Narrowing the view helps users decide faster and adds a playful element to the discovery experience.

ITERATION 03 Recipe card redesign
After

Resized cards so the next recipe peeks into view, encouraging continued scrolling. Added "New" and "Popular" tags to surface noteworthy recipes without overwhelming the layout.

ITERATION 04 Persistent navigation footer
After

Added a persistent navigation footer to secondary pages so users can jump between Home, Saved, Cart, and Account directly — eliminating backtracking and keeping the browsing flow effortless.

14 Reflection

What I learned.

Constrain to clarify. The biggest unlock came from removing options — collapsing the filter tags and offering "Surprise Me" both reduced choices on the surface, but made the experience feel more inviting, not less capable.

Test what matters most. The checkout flow worked smoothly out of the gate, but discovery — the part I'd considered "secondary" — turned out to be where users got stuck. Testing surfaced that early enough to course-correct.

If I had more time… I'd validate the iterations with a follow-up round of usability testing, and explore a lightweight onboarding moment to set expectations before users land on Home for the first time.
